Wrongful dismissal in Ontario: what you may be owed

In Ontario, an employer can usually end someone’s employment without cause, but it must give proper notice or pay instead. Many employees are owed more than the minimum under the Employment Standards Act, 2000, because courts award “reasonable notice” unless a valid termination clause limits it. A wrongful dismissal claim recovers the difference.

Key points

  • Wrongful dismissal usually means not enough notice or pay, not an unfair reason
  • The Employment Standards Act sets minimums; courts often award more
  • Many termination clauses do not hold up in court
  • You generally have two years to sue

What is wrongful dismissal?

Wrongful dismissal does not mean the reason for the firing was unfair. It usually means the employer did not give the notice, or pay in place of notice, that the employee was legally owed. It also covers firings “for cause” where the employer cannot prove just cause, and constructive dismissal, where the employer makes a major change to pay, duties or hours without agreement.

What does the Employment Standards Act require?

The Employment Standards Act, 2000 sets the minimums. After three months of work, employees are owed termination notice or pay of roughly one week per year of service, up to eight weeks. Employees with five or more years of service may also be owed severance pay of one week per year, up to 26 weeks, if the employer’s payroll is $2.5 million or more, or if 50 or more employees lose their jobs within six months because of a closure.

What is “reasonable notice”, and why can it be more?

Unless an employment contract validly limits it, employees are also entitled to common law reasonable notice, which is often far more than the minimums. Courts look at the factors set out in Bardal v. Globe & Mail: age, length of service, the type of position, and how easy it will be to find similar work. Awards range from a few months to around two years in long-service cases.

Does my termination clause hold up?

Often not. Ontario courts read termination clauses strictly in favour of employees. In Waksdale v. Swegon North America (2020), the Court of Appeal held that if any part of the termination provisions breaches the Employment Standards Act, the whole clause can fail, even the part the employer relied on. Clauses that let an employer end employment “at any time” or in its “sole discretion” have also been struck down. A lawyer needs to read the exact wording.

Should I sign the termination letter or release?

Do not sign in a hurry. The amounts owed under the Employment Standards Act must be paid whether you sign or not. A release usually asks you to give up all other claims in exchange for an extra payment, and that offer is often lower than what reasonable notice would give you. Ask for time to get advice; employers usually allow a few days.

What do I need to do after being let go?

  • Keep your contract, offer letter, pay records, the termination letter and any emails about it
  • Look for comparable work and keep a record of your search: the law requires you to try to reduce your loss
  • Note the two-year deadline under the Limitations Act, 2002
  • Decide carefully between a Ministry of Labour complaint and a lawsuit: for the same termination, you generally cannot pursue both

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Am I owed anything if I was fired for cause?

Not if the employer can prove just cause, but that is a high bar. Courts look at the whole context and generally expect serious misconduct. If cause cannot be proven, the employee is owed notice as if they were let go without cause.

Can I sue if I quit because my job was changed?

Possibly. If the employer made a major change to your pay, duties or hours without your agreement, it may be a constructive dismissal. Get advice before resigning, because timing and how you respond matter.

Do wrongful dismissal claims always go to court?

No. Many settle after a demand letter or through negotiation. Starting a claim within the two-year deadline protects your rights while talks continue.
