When a real estate deal fails: deposits, damages and defects

When an Ontario real estate deal falls apart, the Agreement of Purchase and Sale decides most of the outcome. If a buyer fails to close without a legal reason, the seller can usually keep the deposit and sue for further losses, such as a lower resale price. If a seller refuses to close, the buyer can sue for damages and, for a unique property, an order forcing the sale.

Key points

  • A buyer who fails to close usually loses the deposit and can owe more
  • The deposit is held in trust and released by agreement or court order
  • Buyers can sometimes force a sale, but only for a unique property
  • Hidden defects are a claim only in specific situations

Who keeps the deposit when a deal falls through?

The deposit is usually held in trust by the listing brokerage under Ontario’s Trust in Real Estate Services Act, 2002. The brokerage can only release it when both sides sign a mutual release or a court orders it. Where the buyer fails to close without a valid reason, the deposit is generally forfeited to the seller. Where the seller is at fault, or a condition in the agreement was not met, it is generally returned to the buyer.

What can a seller claim when a buyer fails to close?

Beyond the deposit, the seller can claim its actual losses. The most common is the difference between the agreed price and the lower price the property later sells for, plus carrying costs such as mortgage interest, taxes and utilities, minus the deposit kept. The seller must act reasonably to reduce the loss, usually by relisting and selling at fair market value.

What can a buyer do when a seller refuses to close?

The buyer can sue for the return of the deposit and for damages, such as the extra cost of a comparable home. A buyer can also ask for specific performance, an order forcing the sale, but since the Supreme Court of Canada’s decision in Semelhago v. Paramadevan (1996), this is only available where the property is shown to be unique. To stop the seller from selling to someone else while the case continues, the buyer can ask the court for a certificate of pending litigation on title.

Can I sue for defects I discovered after closing?

In Ontario, the general rule for resale homes is “buyer beware”: buyers are expected to inspect for defects. A claim is possible in specific situations, including where:

  • the seller knew of a hidden (latent) defect that made the home dangerous or unfit to live in and did not disclose it
  • the seller actively concealed a defect, for example by covering up water damage
  • the seller made a false statement, including in a Seller Property Information Statement
  • the agreement contained a warranty that turned out to be untrue

What should I do now?

  • Do not sign a mutual release of the deposit until you understand what you give up
  • Keep the agreement, every amendment, your inspection report, photos and all messages with agents
  • Note that “time is of the essence” is standard in Ontario agreements: closing dates are strict
  • Act within the two-year deadline under the Limitations Act, 2002

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Can a buyer walk away if they can’t get financing?

Only if the agreement has a financing condition that has not been waived. Without one, being unable to get a mortgage is generally not a legal reason to refuse to close.

Is the deposit refundable if a condition isn’t met?

Generally yes, if the condition was not fulfilled or waived by the date in the agreement and the agreement becomes void under its own terms. Both sides then usually sign a mutual release.

What does “time is of the essence” mean?

It means the dates in the agreement are strict. A party who is not ready to close on the closing date can be in breach, even if they are only a short time late, unless the other side agrees to an extension in writing.
