Impaired driving & “over 80” charges in Ontario

An impaired driving or “over 80” arrest in Ontario starts two separate processes: immediate provincial penalties at the roadside, including a 90-day licence suspension, and a criminal charge under the Criminal Code that is decided in court. The roadside penalties apply whether or not the charge is later proven, so early legal advice matters.

Key points

  • “Over 80” means 80 mg of alcohol in 100 mL of blood or more within two hours of driving
  • Police can demand a roadside breath sample from any lawfully stopped driver
  • Roadside: 90-day suspension, 7-day impound, $550 penalty and an education course
  • A conviction adds a criminal record, a licence suspension of at least one year and an ignition interlock

What is the difference between impaired driving and “over 80”?

Section 320.14 of the Criminal Code creates several offences. Impaired operation means your ability to drive was impaired to any degree by alcohol, a drug or both. “Over 80” means your blood alcohol concentration was 80 mg of alcohol in 100 mL of blood or more within two hours after you stopped driving. Separate offences cover drug concentrations in the blood. Refusing or failing to provide a breath or blood sample without a reasonable excuse is its own offence under section 320.15, with penalties at least as serious.

Can police demand a breath test without suspecting I was drinking?

Yes. Since December 2018, section 320.27(2) of the Criminal Code allows mandatory alcohol screening: an officer who has an approved screening device can demand a roadside breath sample from any driver who has been lawfully stopped, without any suspicion of drinking. A “fail” usually leads to an arrest and a demand for breath samples on an approved instrument at the police station.

What happens at the roadside in Ontario?

Ontario adds its own administrative penalties on the spot. According to the Ontario government’s impaired driving page (updated June 30, 2026), a driver with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.08 or more, or who fails or refuses a test, faces:

  • an immediate 90-day licence suspension
  • a 7-day vehicle impoundment
  • a $550 penalty and a licence reinstatement fee
  • an 8-hour education course on a first occurrence (a 16-hour treatment program on later ones)

These penalties are not a conviction. They apply before the criminal charge goes to court.

What happens if I am convicted?

A conviction is a criminal record. In Ontario, a first conviction also brings a licence suspension of at least one year, a mandatory education or treatment program, an ignition interlock for at least one year, and then a six-month condition of no alcohol and no drugs while driving. The Criminal Code adds mandatory minimum penalties, including a fine and a driving prohibition, which increase with higher readings and with prior convictions. Insurance costs usually rise sharply after a conviction.

Can an impaired driving charge be defended?

Yes. Every case depends on its evidence, and the Crown must prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. A defence lawyer reviews the disclosure, including the officer’s notes, video and the breath instrument records, and looks at questions such as:

  • Was the stop lawful, and was the breath demand made properly?
  • Were you told of your right to a lawyer and given a real chance to call one?
  • Were the breath samples taken correctly and recorded on an approved instrument?
  • For impaired operation, does the evidence actually show impairment?

A breach of your Charter rights can lead a court to exclude evidence. Whether any of these issues exist can only be answered once the disclosure has been reviewed.

What should I do right now?

  • Keep every document police gave you: the suspension notice, the impound notice and your court date
  • Write down the timeline while you remember it: what you ate and drank, and when
  • Do not discuss the details with anyone except your lawyer, including on social media
  • Never miss your court date; failing to attend is a separate offence

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Is a first impaired driving conviction a criminal record?

Yes. Impaired driving and “over 80” are Criminal Code offences, so a conviction creates a criminal record, even for a first offence.

Can an impaired driving charge affect my immigration status?

It can. Since the 2018 changes raised the maximum penalty, impaired driving is treated as “serious criminality” under the Immigration and Refugee Protection Act. Permanent residents and people on temporary status should get advice from a lawyer who looks at both the criminal and the immigration side.

Does the same apply to cannabis or other drugs?

Yes. Driving while impaired by a drug, or with a prohibited drug concentration in your blood, is a criminal offence. In Ontario, failing a drug evaluation by a trained officer also triggers the 90-day roadside suspension.
