Fraud charges in Ontario: what to know

Fraud under section 380 of the Criminal Code means depriving someone of money, property or a service through deceit, falsehood or other dishonest means. Fraud over $5,000 carries up to 14 years in prison, so anyone in Mississauga who is contacted by police, an employer or a bank about suspected fraud should get legal advice before giving any statement.

Key points

  • The Crown must prove a dishonest act and a loss, or a risk of loss
  • Fraud over $5,000 carries up to 14 years in prison
  • Repaying the money does not erase the offence, but it can matter
  • Get advice before speaking to police or an employer’s investigator

What does the Crown have to prove in a fraud case?

The Supreme Court of Canada set out the test in R. v. Théroux (1993). The Crown must prove a dishonest act, such as a lie, a hidden fact or the unauthorized use of money, and a deprivation, which includes an actual loss or simply putting someone’s financial interests at risk. It must also prove the accused knew about the dishonest act and that it could cause that loss or risk.

What is the difference between fraud under and over $5,000?

Under section 380(1), fraud over $5,000 is an indictable offence with a maximum of 14 years. Fraud under $5,000 can be prosecuted summarily or by indictment, with a maximum of two years on indictment. The value alleged also shapes the Crown’s position on bail and sentencing.

What situations lead to fraud charges?

  • Workplace allegations: expense claims, payroll, inventory or the misuse of company accounts
  • Cheque, e-transfer and credit card cases
  • Online marketplace and rental deposit disputes
  • False information on mortgage, loan or benefit applications
  • Business disputes where one side says the other lied to obtain money

Some of these are really civil disputes about money owed, and the line between a broken promise and a crime is often where a defence begins.

Should I speak to the police or my employer’s investigator?

Not before getting legal advice. A police call inviting you to “give your side” is usually part of an investigation, and anything you say can be used as evidence. You have the right to remain silent and the right to speak to a lawyer. Statements given in an employer’s internal investigation can also reach the police. A lawyer can tell you whether, when and how to respond.

What happens after a fraud charge?

Disclosure in fraud cases is often large: bank records, emails, accounting reports and witness statements. Reviewing it carefully, sometimes with an accountant’s help, is how a defence finds gaps in the paper trail. Many fraud cases resolve through negotiation, and some go to trial.

On sentencing, section 380.1 lists aggravating factors, such as the amount involved, the number of victims and a breach of trust. Courts can also make a restitution order under section 738 requiring repayment. Paying money back before sentencing does not erase the offence, but it can affect the Crown’s position and the sentence.

Can I also be sued?

Yes. The person or company that says it lost money can sue in civil court at the same time, and in larger cases can ask a court to freeze assets. Because the criminal and civil cases affect each other, it helps to have advice on both. Maks Law Firm’s criminal defence and civil litigation lawyers work in the same Mississauga office.

Sources

  • Criminal Code, ss. 380, 380.1, 738
  • R. v. Théroux, [1993] 2 S.C.R. 5

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Can I be charged with fraud if I paid the money back?

Yes. Repayment does not undo the offence if the Crown can prove it. It can, however, matter a great deal in resolution discussions and on sentencing.

Is a risk of loss enough for fraud?

Yes. The Supreme Court of Canada has held that putting someone’s financial interests at risk can be a deprivation, even if no money was ultimately lost.

What is a restitution order?

It is an order made at sentencing under section 738 of the Criminal Code requiring the offender to repay a readily ascertainable loss. It can be enforced like a civil judgment if it is not paid.
