How to recover an unpaid debt in Ontario

When a customer, client or borrower will not pay in Ontario, recovery usually follows three steps: a formal demand letter, a court claim if the demand fails, and enforcement of the judgment through garnishment or seizure if the debtor still does not pay. Claims up to $50,000 go to Small Claims Court; larger debts go to the Superior Court of Justice.

Key points

  • A lawyer’s demand letter settles many debts without court
  • Most debts must be sued on within two years
  • If the debtor does not defend, you can get default judgment
  • Judgments are enforced by garnishment, seizure and examination hearings

Is a demand letter worth sending?

Often, yes. A clear demand letter states the amount owed, the basis for it, a deadline to pay and what will happen next. Many debtors pay or propose a plan at this stage, and the letter becomes part of the record if you need to sue. Keep your tone factual: threats that go beyond legal action can backfire.

What evidence do I need?

  • The contract, quote, purchase order or loan agreement
  • Invoices and statements showing what is owed and when it was due
  • Proof the work was done or the goods were delivered
  • Emails or texts where the debtor accepts the debt or promises to pay

How long do I have to sue?

Under Ontario’s Limitations Act, 2002, the basic deadline is two years, usually from when the payment was due and not made. A signed written acknowledgment of the debt, or a partial payment, can restart the clock under section 13, but it is risky to rely on that without advice.

Which court do I use?

  • Up to $50,000: Small Claims Court, with simpler procedures
  • Up to $200,000 (most claims): the Superior Court of Justice under the Simplified Procedure
  • Larger or complex claims: the Superior Court’s ordinary procedure

If the debtor is served and does not file a defence in time, you can ask for default judgment without a trial.

How is a judgment enforced if they still do not pay?

  • Garnishment: money is taken from the debtor’s bank account or wages, generally up to 20% of net wages under Ontario’s Wages Act
  • Writ of seizure and sale: filed with the sheriff and registered against land, so the debt must be paid when property is sold or refinanced
  • Examination hearing: the debtor must answer questions under oath about income, assets and how they will pay

Judgments also carry post-judgment interest under the Courts of Justice Act until paid.

What should businesses do to avoid unpaid invoices?

Put the payment terms, interest on late accounts and the governing law in writing before the work starts. For business customers, consider deposits, credit applications and personal guarantees from owners, because a corporation is a separate legal person and its owners are not usually liable for its debts. Our business lawyers can review your terms. If the debtor becomes insolvent or files for bankruptcy, a legal stay can stop collection, so act early.

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Can I charge interest on an unpaid invoice?

Yes, at the rate in your contract or invoice terms if the customer agreed to them. Otherwise, the court can award pre-judgment and post-judgment interest at the rates set under the Courts of Justice Act.

Can I sue the owner of a company that owes me money?

Usually only the company is liable, because a corporation is separate from its owners. The owner can be personally liable if they gave the creditor personal guarantees, or in some cases of fraud or wrongdoing.

What if the debtor files for bankruptcy?

A bankruptcy or consumer proposal usually stops collection and lawsuits. You would then file a proof of claim with the licensed insolvency trustee, so get advice as soon as you hear of it.
