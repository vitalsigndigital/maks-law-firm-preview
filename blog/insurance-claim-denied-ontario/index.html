Insurance claim denied? Your options in Ontario

If an insurer denies, delays or underpays a claim in Ontario, the denial letter is not the final word. You can ask for written reasons, use the insurer’s complaint process and an independent ombudservice, and, if needed, sue the insurer or, for car accident benefits, apply to the Licence Appeal Tribunal. Deadlines can be short, so act early.

Key points

  • Ask for the denial and the full policy in writing
  • Complaint routes: the insurer’s ombudsman, then an independent ombudservice
  • Car accident benefit disputes go to the Licence Appeal Tribunal
  • Some deadlines are as short as one year; an internal appeal may not pause them

Why do insurance claims get denied?

Common reasons include a policy exclusion, late notice of the claim, a misstatement on the application, not enough proof of the loss or disability, or a disagreement about how much the loss is worth. Some denials are correct. Others rest on a reading of the policy that does not hold up, or on missing information that can be supplied.

What should I do first?

  • Get the denial in writing, with the specific policy wording the insurer relies on
  • Ask for a full copy of your policy, not just the summary or certificate
  • Ask for your claim file; you are generally entitled to your personal information under federal privacy law
  • Keep every receipt, photo, medical record and letter, and a log of every call

How do I complain about an insurer?

Start with the insurer’s internal complaint process, which usually ends with its ombudsman. If that does not resolve it, independent services can review the complaint at no charge to you: the General Insurance OmbudService for home, auto and business insurance, and the OmbudService for Life & Health Insurance for life, disability and health insurance. Ontario’s regulator, FSRA, also handles complaints about insurer conduct.

What about car accident benefits?

Disputes over statutory accident benefits, such as income replacement, medical and rehabilitation benefits after a car accident, are decided by the Licence Appeal Tribunal, not the courts. An application must generally be made within two years of the insurer’s refusal. A lawsuit against the at-fault driver is a separate claim; see our personal injury page.

How long do I have to sue my insurer?

Many claims fall under the two-year deadline in Ontario’s Limitations Act, 2002, but some are shorter. Some property insurance claims carry a one-year limit under the Insurance Act, and policies can set their own steps and timelines. Going through an internal appeal may not pause the deadline. Get advice on the exact date as soon as a claim is denied.

Can I get more than the policy amount?

Insurers owe their customers a duty of good faith: to handle claims fairly, promptly and in a balanced way. When an insurer breaches that duty, courts can award more than the policy benefits. In Whiten v. Pilot Insurance (2002), the Supreme Court of Canada upheld a large punitive award, and in Fidler v. Sun Life (2006) it allowed damages for mental distress caused by the wrongful denial of disability benefits.

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Should I accept a partial payment from my insurer?

Read what you are signing first. A payment “in full and final settlement” or a signed release can end your claim. A payment of the undisputed part, without a release, usually does not.

Can I sue my own insurance company?

Yes. A policy is a contract, and a policyholder can sue for the benefits owed and, where the insurer acted in bad faith, for additional damages. Accident benefit disputes go to the Licence Appeal Tribunal instead.

Does an internal appeal stop the clock?

Not necessarily. Courts have found that the limitation period can run from the first clear denial, even while an internal appeal is under way. Check the deadline before relying on an appeal.
