Bail hearings in Peel Region: what to expect

In Peel Region, a person who is arrested and held for bail is brought before a justice at the A. Grenville & William Davis Courthouse in Brampton, normally within 24 hours. The justice decides whether the person is released while the case continues, and on what conditions. A clear release plan, often with a surety, is at the centre of most bail hearings.

Key points

  • Bail is decided by a justice, usually within 24 hours of arrest
  • Release on the least onerous conditions is the starting point
  • Since July 15, 2026, more charges carry a reverse onus
  • A surety supervises the accused and pledges money, usually without paying it up front

How quickly does a bail hearing happen after an arrest?

Under section 503 of the Criminal Code, police must bring a person they are holding before a justice without unreasonable delay, and within 24 hours where a justice is available. Many people are never held for bail at all: police can release them with an undertaking or an appearance notice and a first court date.

For arrests in Mississauga and Brampton, bail hearings take place at the Ontario Court of Justice in the A. Grenville & William Davis Courthouse, 7755 Hurontario Street, Brampton, either in person or by video from the police station.

What does the justice decide at a bail hearing?

The justice decides whether the accused can be released before trial and on what terms. Section 515(10) of the Criminal Code allows detention on three grounds only:

  • Primary ground: detention is needed to make sure the person attends court.
  • Secondary ground: detention is needed to protect the public, including the risk of new offences.
  • Tertiary ground: detention is needed to maintain confidence in the administration of justice.

The Supreme Court of Canada has confirmed that release is the default. In R. v. Antic (2017) it described a “ladder” of release options, starting with the least onerous, and in R. v. Zora (2020) it held that every bail condition must be necessary, reasonable and the least onerous that will address the risk.

What is a reverse onus, and what changed in 2026?

Normally the Crown must justify why someone should be detained. In a reverse onus case, the accused must show why release is justified. The federal Bail and Sentencing Reform Act (Bill C-14) came into force on July 15, 2026 and added reverse onus situations, including:

  • auto theft involving violence or organized crime, and breaking into a home
  • assaults and sexual assaults involving choking, suffocating or strangling
  • extortion involving violence, and human trafficking or smuggling
  • serious violent offences where the person has two or more prior convictions for similar offences

Courts must now also consider whether the allegations involve random or unprovoked violence and, on the tertiary ground, the number or seriousness of any outstanding charges. In practice, this makes a well-prepared release plan more important than before.

Who can be a surety, and what do they promise?

A surety is a responsible adult who agrees to supervise the accused while on bail and pledges an amount of money to the court. In Ontario, the surety usually does not pay the money up front; it can be forfeited if the accused breaks the conditions and the surety failed to act. A suitable surety generally:

  • knows the accused well and can supervise them in person, often living with them
  • has stable finances that support the amount pledged
  • has no recent criminal record or outstanding charges
  • understands they must call the police if the accused breaches a condition

Preparing the surety before the hearing matters. The Crown or the justice may question them, and they need to show they understand the role and can actually supervise.

What bail conditions are common?

Typical conditions include living at a set address, a curfew, no contact with named people, staying away from certain places, not possessing weapons and, in more serious cases, house arrest with exceptions. Breaking a condition is a separate offence under section 145 of the Criminal Code and can lead to a new arrest and the loss of bail.

What if bail is denied or the conditions are too strict?

A detention order or unworkable conditions can be challenged through a bail review in the Superior Court of Justice under section 520 of the Criminal Code. Conditions can also be changed later, either with the Crown’s written consent or by a court order, for example when work or family circumstances change.

FAQ

Common questions

General information about Ontario law, not legal advice for your situation.

Does a surety have to pay money up front in Ontario?

Usually not. A surety normally pledges an amount and only pays if the accused breaches the conditions and the surety is found responsible. Cash deposits are required only in limited situations, for example in some cases where the accused lives outside Ontario.

Can bail conditions be changed after the hearing?

Yes. Conditions can be varied with the Crown’s written consent, or by a court on a bail review or variation application. Until a change is made, the original conditions must be followed exactly.

What happens if someone breaks a bail condition?

They can be arrested and charged with failing to comply with a release order under section 145 of the Criminal Code, and their bail can be cancelled. A surety who learns of a breach should contact the police.
